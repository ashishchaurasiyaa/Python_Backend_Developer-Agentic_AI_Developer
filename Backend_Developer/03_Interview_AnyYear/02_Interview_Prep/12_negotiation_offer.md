# Offer Negotiation Guide — Backend Engineer Edition

> Most engineers leave 20-40% on the table by either accepting first offer or negotiating poorly.
> This guide covers Indian market (Tier-1 cities) primarily, with notes on US/global negotiations.

---

## SECTION 1 — MENTAL MODEL: WHY NEGOTIATION IS NOT ADVERSARIAL

**The wrong frame:** "Negotiation = company my enemy, must extract maximum."

**The right frame:** "Both sides want a fair outcome. Recruiters HAVE budget flexibility. Salary bands exist."

Recruiters expect negotiation. **Not negotiating** is a yellow flag — signals you don't know your worth.

**Key insight:** The company has already decided they want you. They sunk 5-15 hours in interviews. Replacing you in the pipeline costs ~30% of your salary. Your leverage is real.

## SECTION 4 — THE NEGOTIATION TIMELINE

```
Phase 1:   Pre-interview        Decide your number, get competing offers
Phase 2:   First salary ask     Recruiter asks expectations — deflect
Phase 3:   Final round done     They make initial offer
Phase 4:   Counter-offer         Push for 15-30% above initial
Phase 5:   Final back-and-forth  Lock in BATNA, get final
Phase 6:   Signing               Get offer in writing, then sign
```

---

## SECTION 5 — PHASE 1: PRE-INTERVIEW PREP

### Decide three numbers BEFORE applying

```
WALK-AWAY (X):     Lowest acceptable. Below this, you say no.
TARGET (Y):        Your goal. Realistic but ambitious.
DREAM (Z):         Best case. What you'd celebrate.
```

**Example for an SDE-2 with 4 years:**
- X = 50 LPA (current + 30% bump)
- Y = 70 LPA (market mid for that band)
- Z = 90 LPA (top 25% of band)

**Rule:** Don't reveal X, Y, or Z. Recruiters anchor on any number you give.

### Get competing offers

The single biggest leverage in negotiation is **a competing offer.** Even a verbal one counts.

**Strategy:** Apply to 5-8 companies in parallel. Sequence interviews so all decisions land within a 1-2 week window. Use early offers as leverage on later ones.

If you can't get parallel offers:
- Use your current salary as anchor — but only if increase is justified.
- Mention specific other companies you're "in process with" (don't lie, but you can be in early stages).

---

## SECTION 6 — PHASE 2: THE "EXPECTATIONS" QUESTION

Recruiter, first call: *"What are your salary expectations?"*

### Worst response
*"60 LPA."* — You just gave them your anchor. They'll offer 55.

### Better response — deflect
> "I'm focused on finding the right role and team fit. Once we've talked through the role and you've decided you want to move forward, I'm sure we can land on something fair. What's the range for this role?"

### If pressed harder
> "Based on what I've seen for similar roles at peer companies, the range is around 70-90 LPA TC. But I'd like to learn more about the role before locking on a number."

### If they reveal a band first
Great. Aim for top of band. Don't accept first number.

---

## SECTION 7 — PHASE 3: THE INITIAL OFFER

The offer comes via call or email. **Don't accept on the spot, even if you're thrilled.**

### Response template (verbal)
> "Thank you so much! This is genuinely exciting. I want to take this seriously, so I'll need a couple of days to review the details with my family and think it through. When do you need a decision by?"

Most recruiters will give you 5-7 days.

### What to ask for in writing
- Base salary.
- Joining bonus (one-time).
- Annual bonus (target % and history of payout).
- Stock grant: total value, vesting schedule, cliff.
- RSU vs ESOP vs Phantom — understand the structure.
- Relocation allowance.
- Notice period flexibility.
- Start date.

### Common offer structures

**Product company (typical):**
- Base: 60% of TC.
- Annual bonus: 10-15% of base.
- Stock: 30% of TC over 4 years (1-yr cliff, then quarterly/monthly).
- Sign-on: 5-15L one-time.

**Late-stage startup:**
- Higher equity, lower base.
- Equity may be ESOP (less liquid than RSU).
- Ask about: liquidity events, secondary sales, dilution.

**Service company:**
- Mostly cash. Little/no equity.
- Annual bonus tied to project utilization.

## SECTION 8 — PHASE 4: THE COUNTER-OFFER

### Rule of three
Always counter on **at least 3 dimensions.** This shows you've thought about the offer, not just being greedy.

**Dimensions to negotiate:**
1. Base salary.
2. Joining bonus.
3. Stock grant.
4. Annual bonus target.
5. Title (e.g., SDE-2 → SDE-3 if borderline).
6. Start date.
7. Relocation.
8. Sign-on stock refresh.

### Sample counter (email)

```
Hi [Recruiter],

Thanks again for the offer at [Company]. I'm genuinely excited about the team
and the work on [specific project mentioned in interview].

I've thought it through and reviewed market data. Based on:

  • My current TC at [Company] of X (verifiable, with payslip)
  • Competing offer from [Company B] at Y total comp
  • Market data from levels.fyi / Blind showing similar roles at Z range

I'd like to discuss the following adjustments:

  1. Base: from 32 LPA → 38 LPA
     (aligns with competing offer + values my Postgres specialization)

  2. Joining bonus: 5L → 10L
     (helps offset the unvested equity I'd leave behind at current employer)

  3. Stock: 60L over 4 yrs → 90L over 4 yrs
     (closer to your published band for SDE-2)

I'm flexible on these — happy to discuss any. The role and team fit are what
matter most, and I think we can land on something that works for both sides.

Let me know a good time to chat.

Best,
[You]
```

### Why this works
- Specific numbers (recruiter can take to HR).
- Backed by data (current + competing + market).
- Three dimensions — gives recruiter options.
- Tone: collaborative, not demanding.
- Closes with willingness to discuss.

## SECTION 9 — COMMON PUSH-BACKS AND HOW TO HANDLE

### "This is our final offer."
*Pause. Then:*
> "I appreciate you saying that. Help me understand — is the constraint the band for this level, the team budget, or something else? I want to know what I'd need to do to close the gap."

Often, "final" means "I can't go higher without escalating." This invites them to escalate.

### "We can't match competitor X's number."
> "I understand. Is there flexibility on the joining bonus or stock side instead? I'd be open to a structure that gets to similar TC even if base is different."

### "We don't negotiate."
This is rare and almost always BS at private companies. (Government/regulated bodies, yes.)
> "I respect that policy. Let me ask — do you have flexibility on level/title? At a higher level, the band itself would resolve the gap."

### "You're being aggressive."
This is a negotiation tactic. Stay calm.
> "I don't mean to come across that way. I'm trying to find a structure that lets me commit fully to this role. What flexibility do you have?"

### "We'll review in 6 months."
A promise of future raise instead of now.
> "I appreciate that. Could we get the 6-month review commitment in writing — both the timing and the target increase? That would help me feel confident moving forward."

(They usually can't put it in writing → real "no" → you negotiate harder now.)

## SECTION 10 — SPECIFIC SCENARIOS

### Scenario A: You have a strong competing offer
**Maximum leverage.** Be specific and verifiable.
> "I have a competing offer at Y LPA from [Company B], joining by [date]. I'd genuinely prefer your team for [reasons], but I need to be within 10% to justify it to my family. Can we close this gap?"

### Scenario B: You have no competing offer
**Anchor on current TC + market.**
> "My current TC is 35 LPA. To make a switch worthwhile — especially leaving unvested stock behind — I'd need around 50 LPA TC. Based on what I've seen for SDE-2 at peer companies, that's mid-band."

### Scenario C: You're a returning candidate (rehire)
**Lower leverage** if you left, then came back. Still negotiate on:
- Refresh of stock (catch up to current grant size).
- Acknowledgment of seniority gain.
- Notice on your current role.

### Scenario D: First job out of college
Bands are narrower. Still negotiate:
- Joining bonus.
- Higher stock if you have a competing offer.
- Start date flexibility.

Don't try to negotiate base by 50% on entry-level — bands are tight.

### Scenario E: Internal promotion vs jumping ship
Numbers from external offer almost always > internal promotion bump.
**Strategy:** Use external offer as leverage internally.
> "I've received an offer at X LPA. I'd prefer to stay if we can get close to that. What can you do?"

But: **only use this if you'd actually leave.** Bluffing damages trust if called.

## SECTION 11 — NEGOTIATING STOCK GRANTS

### Understand what you're getting

| Type | What it is | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| RSU (Restricted Stock Unit) | Real shares, vest over time | Subject to vesting; loss if you leave early |
| ESOP (Stock Option) | Right to buy at strike price | Worth $0 if company doesn't IPO |
| Phantom equity | Cash bonus indexed to stock | No actual stock; just contractual |
| ISOs (US) | Stock options with tax benefits | Time-limited exercise window |

### Questions to ask
- What's the current 409A valuation (private) or share price (public)?
- Total grant size in shares + USD equivalent.
- Vesting schedule (1-year cliff standard, then monthly/quarterly).
- Refresh policy — do top performers get annual refreshes?
- What happens to unvested at exit (IPO, acquisition, layoff)?
- Tax implications at vest vs sale.

### Red flags
- Refusal to share grant size in $.
- Vague vesting ("we'll decide later").
- Strike price > current 409A (negative immediate value).
- No refresh policy.

### Convert grants for apples-to-apples
4-year RSU grant: divide by 4 → annual value.
ESOP: discount by 30-50% (illiquidity, dilution risk).
Phantom: discount 20% (vesting still applies).

## SECTION 12 — SUBTLE NEGOTIATION TACTICS

### The pause
After their initial offer, say "I see..." and stay silent for 5 seconds. Many recruiters will fill the silence with a small concession.

### The "wow"
> "Wow. That's lower than I expected. Help me understand the gap."

Forces them to justify, not you.

### The flinch
A visible (or audible) reaction of surprise/disappointment when the number lands. Done sparingly. Genuine reactions matter.

### Reframe to value
> "If I look at my last 3 years' impact — [X, Y, Z] — and the salary you're proposing puts me at the 25th percentile for SDE-2. Given my experience, mid-band feels more accurate."

### The escalation request
> "What if we got the hiring manager involved? I'd love to understand from them what flexibility exists."

Hiring manager has more incentive to close (they need the headcount). Recruiter is gatekeeper.

### The future-anchor
> "If I joined and proved my impact in 6 months, what would the comp look like a year from now?"

Use this to extract a written commitment for first-year refresh.

## SECTION 13 — DON'TS

### Don't lie about competing offers
Recruiters sometimes verify (you'd be shocked). If exposed, offer rescinded. Career hit.

### Don't negotiate via email-only
Calls move faster. Email creates negotiation paper trail (good for record, bad for tone).

### Don't reveal current salary if you don't have to
In many states/countries it's illegal to ask. Even where legal, deflect.
> "I'd prefer to focus on what the role is worth in your market rather than anchor on my current numbers."

### Don't accept verbally before written offer arrives
"Verbal yes" = recruiter stops negotiating. Wait for written → then negotiate further if needed.

### Don't burn bridges
Even if you walk away, do it gracefully. Industry is small.
> "This role isn't right for me at this comp, but I deeply appreciate your time. I'd love to stay in touch."

### Don't accept the same day
At least sleep on it. Decisions made in the heat of negotiation often have regret.

### Don't disclose your other-offer numbers verbatim
> "I have a competing offer that's competitive at the senior level..."
*Not:* "Razorpay is offering me 75 LPA, and I want you to match."

## SECTION 14 — THE FINAL ACCEPTANCE

When you've negotiated as far as you can and want to accept:

### Get everything in writing first
- Final offer letter.
- Total compensation breakdown.
- Stock grant agreement.
- Joining bonus + claw-back terms (if any).
- Start date.
- Any verbal promises made.

### Read the offer letter carefully
- **Claw-back clauses:** Joining bonus often returnable if you leave within 12-24 months.
- **Non-compete:** Mostly unenforceable in India, but check.
- **IP assignment:** Standard, but understand it.
- **Mandatory arbitration:** Usually present, low impact.

### Final acceptance email
```
Hi [Recruiter],

I'm thrilled to accept the offer for [Role] at [Company], effective [Start Date].

Confirming the package:
  • Base salary: X LPA
  • Joining bonus: Y L
  • Annual bonus target: Z%
  • Stock grant: $A over 4 years, [vesting schedule]
  • Relocation allowance: $B

Looking forward to joining the team and contributing to [specific project].

I'll be in touch about [resignation timeline / paperwork / start date logistics].

Best,
[You]
```

### Resign professionally
- 2-4 weeks notice standard (India: 30-90 days per contract).
- Don't go on a victory lap; you'll be remembered.
- Negotiate notice down if possible.
- Knowledge transfer thoroughly.

## SECTION 15 — COUNTER-OFFERS FROM CURRENT EMPLOYER

When you give notice, your current employer may counter.

### Statistics (anecdotal but consistent)
- ~50% of engineers who accept counter-offer leave within 12 months anyway.
- Trust is broken — managers know you're a flight risk.
- Underlying reasons you wanted to leave often haven't changed.

### When counter-offer makes sense
- The underlying issue is *only* money (rare).
- New offer is materially better (>30% bump) and you can extract similar from current.
- You genuinely want to stay but were under-paid.

### When counter-offer doesn't make sense
- You were leaving for reasons beyond money (manager, growth, product).
- Increase is mid (10-15%).
- New employer is a step up in brand/learning.

### Handle counter-offer gracefully
> "I appreciate the gesture deeply. Honestly, I've thought about this a lot and the decision isn't just about comp. [Reason X]. I'll be moving forward with [new company], but I'm grateful for the years here and want to make sure my transition is clean."

## SECTION 16 — LONG-TERM NEGOTIATION (RAISES AT CURRENT JOB)

Negotiation isn't just at joining. Every annual review is a mini-negotiation.

### Build the case throughout the year
- Track wins in a "brag doc."
- Quantify impact ($ saved, latency reduced, engineers mentored).
- Schedule 1:1 conversations about growth path.

### When to ask
- After a successful project ship.
- After taking on extra responsibility.
- At the formal review cycle.

### What to ask for
- Base bump (typical: 8-15% on internal promotion).
- Stock refresh (often forgotten — top performers get refreshes).
- Title change (signals seniority externally).
- Scope expansion (tech lead role, more autonomy).

### When raise is denied
> "I understand. What would I need to do to be at the next level by [date]? Can we document those expectations now?"

This creates accountability for both sides.

## FINAL THOUGHTS

### The asymmetry
- The 1-2 weeks of negotiation effort: a few hours total.
- The salary increase: persists for years.
- ROI on negotiation time: typically thousands of dollars per hour.

### Mindset
You are not asking for a favor. You are negotiating a business arrangement between two parties with mutual interest. The recruiter expects this. The hiring manager expects this. **Behaving as if you don't deserve to be paid well is the only way to get underpaid.**

### When in doubt
- Ask. The worst outcome is "no."
- Get it in writing.
- Sleep on it before signing.

**Aim high. Be polite. Be willing to walk away.** That's the formula.
